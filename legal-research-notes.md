# Silara Marketing legal research notes

## Working status and limitations

These notes support preparation of general B2B website terms and privacy-policy drafts. They are not legal advice and do not replace review by an Australian lawyer. The final documents must not claim or imply guaranteed compliance with the Privacy Act, Australian Consumer Law, NDIS rules, aged-care law, health-records legislation, or any state or territory requirement.

## OAIC and Australian Privacy Principles

**Sources reviewed 17 July 2026:**

1. OAIC, *Australian Privacy Principles quick reference*: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-quick-reference
2. OAIC, *Chapter 1: APP 1 Open and transparent management of personal information*: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-1-app-1-open-and-transparent-management-of-personal-information

The APP framework requires open and transparent management of personal information. APP 1 includes a clearly expressed and up-to-date privacy policy. The official quick reference also identifies the core lifecycle requirements relevant to Silara: appropriate collection, notification at collection, limited use and disclosure, direct-marketing conditions, cross-border disclosure safeguards, reasonable security, destruction or de-identification where appropriate, and individual access and correction rights.

OAIC guidance says an APP privacy policy should be easy to understand, easy to navigate, relevant to the entity's information handling, suitable for web publication, kept up to date, and reviewed regularly. It should explain the entity's personal-information flows without trying to reproduce every internal privacy procedure. A privacy policy is distinct from an APP 5 collection notice, so Silara's website forms and future Wufoo embeds should also carry context-specific collection notices.

The OAIC page flags additional privacy-policy obligations concerning substantially automated decisions commencing in December 2026. Silara should therefore avoid asserting that its 2026 policy is permanently complete and should schedule legal review before those changes commence, especially for products using AI-assisted analysis, prioritisation, or recommendations.

## ACCC, Australian Consumer Law, and B2B standard-form contracts

**Sources reviewed 17 July 2026:**

3. ACCC, *Contracts*: https://www.accc.gov.au/business/selling-products-and-services/contracts
4. ACCC, *Businesses urged to remove unfair contract terms ahead of law changes*: https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/businesses-urged-to-remove-unfair-contract-terms-ahead-of-law-changes

The ACCC states that Australian Consumer Law protections can affect standard-form contracts with small businesses even where the supply is described as B2B. Since 9 November 2023, proposing, using, or relying on unfair terms in covered standard-form consumer and small-business contracts is prohibited and may attract penalties. Silara's terms therefore should not rely on a blanket statement that all Australian Consumer Law protections are excluded merely because customers are businesses.

The official ACCC guidance recommends considering both parties' positions, using counter-balancing rights, limiting clauses to what is reasonably necessary to protect legitimate interests, avoiding attempts to exclude mandatory consumer guarantees or outside representations, writing clearly, and drawing key terms to the customer's attention at sign-up and renewal. For Silara, unilateral product or pricing changes should be accompanied by notice and a reasonable exit right where the change is materially detrimental. Suspension, indemnity, liability, automatic renewal, data-use, and termination rights should be proportionate, mutual where appropriate, and transparent rather than drafted as unlimited one-way powers.

The terms should preserve any non-excludable rights and state that limitations operate only to the maximum extent permitted by law. They should also separate the public website terms from any negotiated order form, service schedule, data-processing terms, or product-specific agreement, with a clear order of precedence.

## Consumer guarantees and product representations

**Sources reviewed 17 July 2026:**

5. ACCC, *Consumer rights and guarantees*: https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/consumer-rights-and-guarantees
6. ACCC, *False or misleading claims*: https://www.accc.gov.au/business/advertising-and-promotions/false-or-misleading-claims

ACCC guidance confirms that consumer guarantees are automatic and cannot be removed by contract when they apply. Service guarantees include due care and skill, fitness for a disclosed purpose, and supply within a reasonable time where no time is fixed. Because some business purchasers may still be treated as consumers for particular acquisitions, Silara's B2B terms must preserve non-excludable guarantees and remedies rather than using an absolute warranty exclusion.

The ACCC also states that claims must be true, accurate, and based on reasonable grounds; the business must be able to substantiate advertised claims. Silence can mislead where important information is omitted. Silara's terms and responsible-use notices should therefore align with the website's proof-before-promises approach: products should not be represented as guaranteeing compliance, suitability, clinical accuracy, vacancy fills, incident closure, worker eligibility, reputation outcomes, or regulatory acceptance. Early-access status, human-review requirements, material limits, and customer responsibilities should be clear before contracting, not hidden in fine print.

## NDIS provider and incident-management findings

**Sources reviewed 17 July 2026:**

7. NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission, *Incident management*: https://www.ndiscommission.gov.au/rules-and-standards/reportable-incidents-and-incident-management/incident-management
8. NDIA, *What are the responsibilities of a provider*: https://www.ndis.gov.au/providers/working-provider/provider-responsibilities/what-are-responsibilities-provider

The NDIS Commission says all NDIS providers should have an incident-management system and registered providers must have one as a registration condition. The provider remains responsible for making affected people safe, identifying and recording incidents and evidence, protecting privacy and confidentiality, assessing and investigating events, deciding whether others or the Commission must be notified, reporting where required, and applying learnings. IncidentIQ can support workflow and evidence continuity but must not be represented as replacing the provider's documented system, emergency response, professional judgement, reportability assessment, investigation, statutory notification, or participant involvement.

NDIA guidance confirms that providers must comply with Australian Consumer Law and retain responsibility for pricing disclosures to participants, accurate records, invoicing, payment requests, conflicts, registration requirements, and compliance with applicable NDIS Practice Standards and the Code of Conduct. The Terms should make each customer responsible for its participant agreements, service representations, records, billing, registration status, worker conduct, conflicts, restrictive-practice obligations, regulatory submissions, and service quality. Silara should be described as a B2B technology and marketing supplier, not as the NDIS provider, support coordinator, health professional, decision-maker, or regulator.

## Aged-care provider and information-management findings

**Sources reviewed 17 July 2026:**

9. Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission, *Quality Standards*: https://www.agedcarequality.gov.au/providers/quality-standards
10. Department of Health, Disability and Ageing, *Guide to Aged Care Law — Chapter 7: Information management*: https://www.health.gov.au/resources/publications/guide-to-aged-care-law/chapter-7-information-management?language=en

The strengthened Aged Care Quality Standards have applied since 1 November 2025. They are rights-based and place organisational accountability on providers and their governing bodies across the individual, the organisation, care and services, environment, clinical care, food and nutrition, and residential community. Silara's products may support operational systems and evidence, but they do not deliver or supervise care, make clinical decisions, satisfy governing-body responsibilities, or certify conformance with the Standards.

Chapter 7 of the Aged Care Act 2024 contains rules for managing and protecting information, using and disclosing protected information, keeping records, public information, and whistleblower protections, with penalties for misuse. Silara's Terms should require customers to ensure they have lawful authority to upload and instruct Silara to process aged-care information, configure role-based access, protect credentials, apply retention and disclosure rules, preserve whistleblower protections, and maintain any records or reports required of the provider. Silara should offer contractual confidentiality and security commitments without claiming that its general website terms alone satisfy all Commonwealth, state, or territory health-records requirements.

## Health information and Privacy Act coverage

**Source reviewed 17 July 2026:**

11. OAIC, *Small business*: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business

The OAIC explains that, although many businesses with annual turnover of $3 million or less may fall outside the Privacy Act, important exceptions apply regardless of turnover. These include health service providers, businesses trading in personal information, Commonwealth contractors, businesses related to covered entities, prescribed businesses, and businesses that opt in. Silara should therefore adopt APP-aligned privacy practices as the contractual baseline and should not promise that it is exempt merely because of size. The final lawyer review should determine Silara's exact APP-entity status, including whether any product activity constitutes providing a health service, trading in personal information, operating in a related body corporate group, or performing a Commonwealth contract.

## Data breaches and direct marketing

**Sources reviewed 17 July 2026:**

12. OAIC, *Part 4: Notifiable Data Breach Scheme*: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/notifiable-data-breaches/preventing-preparing-for-and-responding-to-data-breaches/data-breach-preparation-and-response/part-4-notifiable-data-breach-ndb-scheme
13. OAIC, *Direct marketing*: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/direct-marketing

The NDB Scheme applies to entities with Privacy Act security obligations, including covered organisations, private-sector health service providers, entities trading in personal information, and other specified entities. An eligible data breach involves unauthorised access, disclosure, or loss where serious harm is likely and remedial action has not removed that likelihood. Covered entities must assess suspected eligible breaches and, where notification is required, notify the OAIC and affected individuals. Silara's policy should describe incident assessment and legally required notification without promising a fixed statutory result or treating every security incident as automatically notifiable. Customer contracts should require prompt cooperation where a breach affects customer-controlled information.

APP 7 regulates use or disclosure of personal information for direct marketing and interacts with the Spam Act and Do Not Call Register Act. Silara should provide simple opt-out mechanisms, honour opt-out requests, avoid using sensitive information for marketing without appropriate consent, and explain the source or use of marketing data where required. Website and future Wufoo collection notices should distinguish enquiry follow-up necessary to respond to a request from optional marketing communications.

**Additional source reviewed 17 July 2026:**

14. ACMA, *Avoid sending spam*: https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam

ACMA guidance requires commercial electronic messages to be sent with the required consent, accurately identify the sender, provide correct contact details, and include a clear unsubscribe facility. Sender details and the unsubscribe facility must remain functional for at least 30 days after sending; unsubscribe requests must be honoured within five working days, without a fee, unusual cost, login, account creation, or demand for additional personal information. Silara remains responsible when a third-party platform sends messages on its behalf. The privacy policy and form notices should therefore separate service communications from optional marketing and explain how recipients can withdraw marketing consent.

## Health and allied-health information

**Source reviewed 17 July 2026:**

15. OAIC, *Guide to Health Privacy*, version 2.0, May 2025: https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0020/251183/Guide-to-Health-Privacy-Collated-May-2025.pdf

The OAIC guide states that health-service providers include allied-health professionals, private aged-care and palliative-care facilities, disability-service providers where they handle health information, and health services delivered online. All personal information collected in the course of providing a health service can be health information, and health information is sensitive information subject to stricter handling requirements. It can include clinical information, disability or injury information, service information, billing and appointment details, identifiers, and other personal details collected to provide a health service.

Consent for health-information handling must be informed, voluntary, current, specific, and given by a person with capacity, whether express or validly implied. De-identification requires more than removing direct identifiers; re-identification risk must be actively assessed against available data and circumstances. The Privacy Policy and Terms should therefore require each customer to determine and document its lawful collection and disclosure authority, provide required notices, manage consent and representatives, apply access controls, minimise uploaded information, and avoid placing unnecessary participant, resident, client, or patient data into marketing forms. Silara should process customer content only for agreed B2B services and documented instructions, subject to legal exceptions, without assuming the customer's clinical or care-provider obligations.
